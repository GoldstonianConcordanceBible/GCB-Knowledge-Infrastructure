# Model SSCM 101 Artifact
## Supply Chain Map Explanation

**Title:** Mapping a Packaged Food Supply Chain  
**Course:** SSCM 101 — Foundations of Sustainable Supply Chains

---

## 1. Overview

This supply chain begins with agricultural inputs, moves through processing and packaging, then passes through warehousing, transportation, retail distribution, and customer purchase. Although the product appears simple on the shelf, the underlying system depends on multiple suppliers, packaging partners, logistics providers, and seasonal conditions.

---

## 2. Most Fragile Point

The most fragile point is the packaging stage.

Even if food inputs are available, the final product cannot move to retail if packaging materials are delayed. This means a non-food input can become the bottleneck for the entire chain.

---

## 3. Highest Ethical or Labor Risk

The highest ethical risk appears in agricultural sourcing and low-visibility supplier labor conditions.

The organization may have stronger control over downstream warehousing and retail relationships than over upstream labor conditions, especially if raw inputs come through layered supplier networks.

---

## 4. Largest Environmental Concern

The largest environmental concern is likely a combination of transport emissions and packaging waste.

A full sustainability analysis must therefore look beyond production and examine how shipping frequency, distance, and packaging design affect the total system.

---

## 5. Weakest Visibility Point

Visibility is weakest upstream.

Organizations often know more about internal inventory and retail fulfillment than about the labor, sourcing, and environmental conditions embedded in early supplier tiers.

---

## 6. Redesign Recommendation

A realistic redesign would begin with supplier-tier mapping and packaging-risk reduction.

This recommendation is practical because it improves both resilience and sustainability.
It also avoids the common mistake of treating environmental improvement and operational improvement as separate goals.

---

## 7. Why This Matters

This example shows that a supply chain should be understood as a connected system of dependencies.
The point of mapping is not only to describe the chain, but to identify where leadership lacks visibility, where risk is concentrated, and where redesign creates the most value.
