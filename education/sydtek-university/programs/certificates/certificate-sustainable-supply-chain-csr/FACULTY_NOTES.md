# Faculty Notes
## Certificate in Sustainable Supply Chain Management & Corporate Social Responsibility

This document provides teaching guidance for faculty, mentors, and program leads using this certificate.

---

## 1. Teaching Posture

Faculty should teach this certificate with a balance of:

- practical realism
- ethical seriousness
- clarity
- systems thinking
- restraint around hype

Students should leave with better judgment, not just better vocabulary.

---

## 2. Core Teaching Emphasis

Across all four courses, instructors should repeatedly emphasize:

### Sustainability is operational.
It must show up in sourcing, labor, logistics, data, governance, and decision-making.

### CSR is not marketing.
Students should learn to distinguish symbolic responsibility from embedded responsibility.

### Technology is conditional.
Students should not assume AI, blockchain, or digital twins are automatically the right answer.

### Resilience requires tradeoffs.
Cheap systems can be brittle.
Transparent systems may require investment.
Responsible systems often require redesign.

---

## 3. Common Student Errors to Watch For

### A. Confusing slogans with systems
Students may repeat language like sustainability, ethics, or ESG without operational detail.

### B. Assuming more data solves everything
Students may overestimate dashboards and underestimate data quality problems.

### C. Treating CSR as public relations
Push students to test whether responsibility is real inside the operating model.

### D. Recommending unrealistic transformation
Students often propose too much change at once.
Require prioritization.

### E. Ignoring labor and governance
Students may focus on technology and forget the human and governance layers.

---

## 4. Teaching Through Cases

This certificate works best when grounded in:

- disruption cases
- supplier failure cases
- governance breakdowns
- sustainability controversies
- logistics redesign cases
- technology implementation examples

Faculty should prefer applied examples over abstract lectures alone.

---

## 5. Portfolio Standards

Faculty should remind students that portfolio artifacts must be:

- clear enough for outside review
- well-organized
- evidence-based
- practical
- suitable for academic or professional presentation

---

## 6. Scholars Adaptation

For SydTek Scholars, instructors may shorten outputs while preserving rigor.

Adaptations may include:

- shorter memos
- guided templates
- more scaffolded discussions
- simplified cases
- presentation-based alternatives

Do not dilute the seriousness of the ideas.

---

## 7. Feedback Priorities

Feedback should prioritize:

1. clarity of thought
2. realism of recommendation
3. strength of reasoning
4. awareness of tradeoffs
5. quality of communication

---

## 8. Capstone Guidance

In the capstone, faculty should push students to answer five questions:

1. What exactly is broken?
2. Why does it matter?
3. What should be fixed first?
4. What is realistic now?
5. How will success be measured?

These questions keep the capstone grounded.

---

## 9. Recommended Faculty Additions Over Time

As the program matures, faculty may add:

- sample cases
- example portfolios
- short lecture scripts
- sample feedback language
- moderation guidance
- employer review notes
